## 2. Shared Documentation-Level Hierarchy Contract

This section defines the shared documentation-level hierarchy contract for future Market and Neighborhood discovery work.

Market and Neighborhood discovery should use the following shared documentation-level hierarchy:

1. Place orientation
2. Governing customer question
3. Concise customer promise
4. Scope-specific context themes
5. Tool or evidence continuation
6. Questions to verify
7. Evidence, freshness, limitation, and professional boundaries
8. Transition to Search, Property, Buyer, Seller, or Advisory journey
9. Compact next-decision continuations

This is not a shared runtime abstraction, schema, or component framework. It is a documentation-level contract for future separately authorized runtime phases.

## 3. Market Experience Contract

Market pages as customer briefings, not governance reports.

Future Market pages should help customers answer:

- What is happening in this market?
- What evidence matters to my decision?
- What is directional versus verified?
- What should I investigate next?
- Which property or neighborhood journey should I continue into?

Market-specific requirements:

- lead with the customer decision question before dense visual intelligence or stats;
- explain the strongest current signals in plain language;
- distinguish directional market context from verified property-specific evidence;
- place freshness and limitation language adjacent to market claims;
- route customers toward Search, Neighborhood, Property, Buyer, Seller, or Advisory next steps;
- keep seller preparation context separate from valuation certainty;
- keep buyer preparation context separate from qualification or affordability conclusions.

Market pages should be concise customer briefings.

Market pages must be not an internal KPI dashboard and not a forecast, valuation, ranking, investment recommendation, or predictive certainty engine.

## 4. Neighborhood Experience Contract

Neighborhood pages as orientation to place before evidence.

Future Neighborhood pages should help customers answer:

- What kind of place is this?
- How is it organized geographically?
- What housing context is available?
- What should I verify personally or professionally?
- Which properties, markets, or advisory resources should I explore next?

Neighborhood-specific requirements:

- establish place identity before dense inventory, market, visual-intelligence, or evidence sections;
- explain geography and local organization without implying suitability;
- place housing context after orientation and before deeper evidence;
- route to Search, city Market, nearby Neighborhoods, Property pages, Seller, Buyer, or Advisory as appropriate;
- place verification prompts near claims that require professional, municipal, HOA, insurance, title, inspection, or personal review;
- preserve sparse and fallback states without turning gaps into conclusions.

Neighborhood pages must preserve fair-housing boundaries:

- no protected-class steering;
- no demographic suitability conclusions;
- no best-neighborhood claims;
- no safety guarantees;
- no school-quality conclusions;
- no investment guarantees;
- no predictive appreciation claims;
- no personalized suitability rankings.

## 5. Evidence And Limitation Placement

Evidence, limitations, freshness, and professional boundaries should appear at the moment the customer is asked to rely on context.

Required placement rules:

- Market signal limitations belong near market direction, pricing context, competitiveness, timing, and inventory claims;
- Neighborhood limitation language belongs near place, inventory, geographic, school-adjacent, commute, condition, and lifestyle context;
- Search continuation should follow enough context for the customer to know why they are searching;
- Property continuation should distinguish place-level context from property-specific verification;
- Advisory continuation should appear after unresolved questions and boundaries are visible.
